Proposal for NNC0114-0006, a fully human monoclonal antibody targeting interleukin-21 (IL-21) (Novozymes Biopharma, IMGT-aligned sequence available).

Below is our comprehensive evaluation of NNC0114-0006 as a potential therapeutic candidate for type 1 diabetes.

Overview of Therapeutic Candidate:  
NNC0114-0006 is a fully human monoclonal antibody developed by Novozymes Biopharma that is designed to target interleukin-21 (IL-21), a cytokine with a well‐documented role in modulating immune responses. This antibody—whose amino acid sequence has been IMGT‐aligned—belongs to the class of biologic immunomodulators. Monoclonal antibodies such as this one are typically generated using recombinant technologies that allow for the selection of antibody clones with high affinity and specificity toward a target antigen. In this case, the target is IL-21, a cytokine produced primarily by CD4+ T follicular helper (Tfh) cells (Buniello et al., 2025). Extensive research has established that IL-21 contributes to the pathogenesis of type 1 diabetes (T1D) through its effects on T- and B-cell functions, and by promoting a proinflammatory milieu. The therapeutic candidate is conceptually similar to other cytokine-targeting agents that have been used in autoimmunity, although previous approaches for T1D—such as anti-CD3 or anti-CD20 antibodies—have had limited or transient effects. NNC0114-0006 is unique because it directly neutralizes IL-21, thus aiming to specifically interfere with an immune pathway that is genetically and functionally implicated in T1D (Buniello et al., 2025).

Therapeutic History:  
Antibody-based biologics have a well established history in managing autoimmune or inflammatory diseases. In the context of T1D, several classes of immune modulators (e.g., anti-CD3, anti-CD20 agents) have been trialed with varying degrees of success; however, none have yet led to sustained preservation of β-cell function without significant adverse effects. Preclinical studies in non-obese diabetic (NOD) mouse models have demonstrated that IL-21 is a pivotal driver of β-cell destruction, and genetic ablation or receptor knockout of IL-21R confers protection from diabetes onset (Grunnet & Mandrup-Poulsen, 2011; Buniello et al., 2025). Indeed, IL-21 has been shown both genetically and functionally to be necessary for autoimmune diabetes progression in these models. More recently, early-phase clinical work—including single-dose studies with favorable pharmacokinetics (with estimates of a half-life around 14 days) and safety profiles—has provided preliminary support for advancing anti-IL-21 therapies into the clinic (Buniello et al., 2025). Although this antibody class has yet to be approved for T1D, ongoing phase 2 trials, including studies combining anti-IL-21 with liraglutide, have provided translational proof‐of‐concept evidence that IL-21 blockade can positively affect markers of β-cell preservation (Buniello et al., 2025).

Mechanism of Action:  
The pathophysiology of T1D involves autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β cells mediated by dysregulated T-cell responses. IL-21 is a cytokine that signals through the IL-21 receptor (IL-21R), engaging key downstream effectors such as Janus kinase (JAK)/STAT pathways (with a particularly notable role for STAT3), MAPK, and PI3K cascades (Buniello et al., 2025). IL-21 is produced principally by activated CD4+ Tfh and Th17 cells, and its engagement with IL-21R on target cells—such as effector CD8+ T cells and B cells—leads to expansion, enhanced survival, and increased effector functions. This includes the production of inflammatory cytokines such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and up-regulation of cytotoxic proteins (e.g., granzyme B), which are implicated in the destruction of β cells (Buniello et al., 2025). NNC0114-0006 functions by binding IL-21 with high affinity, thereby preventing its interaction with IL-21R. This blockade reduces the downstream proliferation and survival signals required for the maintenance of autoreactive Tfh cells and CD8+ effector T cells. In preclinical adoptive transfer models using NOD mice, treatment with NNC0114-0006 (administered at doses around 20 mg/kg IV weekly) led to more than an 80% reduction in Tfh cell frequency, a decrease in islet autoantibody levels, and a delay in diabetes onset by approximately 75% (Buniello et al., 2025). In humanized mouse models employing patient-derived T cells, blocking IL-21 yielded significant reductions in IFN-γ production and CD8+ cell granzyme B release in in vitro co-culture systems with human islets (Buniello et al., 2025; Schroderus et al., 2023). These data support a detailed molecular mechanism in which neutralization of IL-21 mitigates the autoreactive immune cascade by directly limiting T-cell expansion and effector function, thereby potentially preserving the viability of β cells (Rydén et al., 2017; von Herrath et al., 2021).

Expected Effect:  
Based on preclinical findings and initial humanized assays, the expected therapeutic effect of NNC0114-0006 is twofold. First, by neutralizing IL-21, the antibody is thought to reduce the IL-21R–dependent expansion and survival of autoreactive Tfh cells that foster germinal center formation and subsequent anti-islet autoantibody generation. This reduction in pathogenic B-cell help is predicted to culminate in lower titers of autoantibodies that are involved in the immune-mediated destruction of β cells (Buniello et al., 2025). Second, the antibody is expected to diminish the expansion and activation of effector CD8+ T cells that are directly responsible for cytotoxic β-cell killing. In humanized systems, blockade of IL-21 has been shown to lower IFN-γ production and decrease granzyme-B release from CD8+ T cells, both of which are crucial for mediating pancreatic islet damage (von Herrath et al., 2021; Buniello et al., 2025). In combination with a GLP-1 receptor agonist such as liraglutide—which itself improves β-cell survival through reduced endoplasmic reticulum stress and enhanced proinsulin processing—the expected outcome is the preservation of residual β-cell mass and function in recent-onset T1D. Clinically, these effects would be measurable as a slower decline in meal-stimulated C-peptide secretion, reduced exogenous insulin requirements, and improved glycemic control without causing broad immunosuppression (Rydén et al., 2017; “Preservation of Beta Cell Function,” n.d.). Additionally, the favorable pharmacokinetic properties (t½ ~14 days) suggest that weekly or biweekly dosing may maintain effective IL-21 neutralization, aligning with the preclinical efficacies observed in NOD mouse adoptive transfer models (Rydén et al., 2017; von Herrath et al., 2021).

Overall Evaluation:  
Overall, NNC0114-0006 represents a promising therapeutic candidate for T1D based on a robust combination of genetic, immunologic, and preclinical evidence. One of the core strengths of this candidate is the strong validation of the IL-21/IL-21R pathway in T1D pathogenesis. Genetic association studies and murine knockout models have consistently shown that IL-21 is a critical cytokine that drives autoreactive T-cell responses and β-cell destruction (Buniello et al., 2025). The preclinical efficacy data are particularly compelling: a marked reduction in Tfh cell frequency, decreased titers of islet autoantibodies, and significant delays in diabetes onset in adoptive transfer NOD models provide a solid proof-of-concept that targeting IL-21 can modulate the underlying autoimmune process (Rydén et al., 2017). In humanized mouse models, the observed decrease in IFN-γ and granzyme B production reinforces the candidate’s potential to suppress cytotoxic T-cell activity against β cells (Schroderus et al., 2023).

In terms of pharmacokinetics, the favorable half-life of approximately 14 days lends support to a dosing regimen that is practical and patient-friendly, potentially allowing for sustained IL-21 blockade with intermittent dosing (von Herrath et al., 2021). Moreover, initial safety signals from single-dose studies indicate that NNC0114-0006 does not induce broad or prolonged immunosuppression—a critical consideration given the delicate balance of immune regulation in T1D and the risk of opportunistic infections (von Herrath et al., 2021).

Nonetheless, there are several weaknesses and challenges that must be considered. Although anti-IL-21 monotherapy has demonstrated a substantial immunomodulatory effect in preclinical models, data suggest that its use in reversing established diabetes may be limited unless combined with supportive interventions such as liraglutide (Rydén et al., 2017). Combination therapy appears necessary to achieve both immunomodulation and direct β-cell support, a strategy that, while promising, may complicate dosing, increase costs, and potentially introduce additional regulatory hurdles. Furthermore, the long-term safety of chronic IL-21 blockade remains to be determined. Although early studies indicate that immune perturbations are mild and transient, IL-21 also plays roles in normal host defense and immunosurveillance, and its prolonged inhibition could conceivably alter responses to infectious agents or malignancy (“Preservation of Beta Cell Function,” n.d.; von Herrath et al., 2021).

Another potential limitation is the transient nature of the immune modulation observed in preclinical studies; the protective effects of anti-IL-21 treatment may wane upon cessation, which suggests that continuous administration might be required for sustained benefit (von Herrath et al., 2021). This raises important questions regarding treatment duration, long-term adherence, and cost–benefit considerations in a chronic disease setting. Additionally, it remains to be clarified whether the reduction in Tfh cell frequency and CD8+ cytotoxic activity will translate into clinically meaningful preservation of endogenous insulin secretion in the heterogeneous human T1D population. The immune system’s redundancy in cytokine networks means that compensatory pathways could offset some of the benefits of IL-21 blockade (Grunnet & Mandrup-Poulsen, 2011; ClinicalTrials.gov, 2015).

In summary, the accumulating evidence supports the hypothesis that neutralizing IL-21 with NNC0114-0006 can modulate key immunological pathways implicated in T1D. The candidate’s ability to substantially reduce Tfh cell frequency and downstream signatures of autoimmune activation in preclinical models, together with favorable pharmacokinetic and safety profiles, provides a strong rationale for further clinical development. However, the candidate’s full potential may be best realized in combination with agents such as liraglutide, which not only enhance β-cell survival but also potentially synergize with immunomodulatory effects to achieve better clinical outcomes. Ongoing and future clinical trials will be essential to validate these preclinical findings, elucidate the long-term impact on β-cell preservation, and determine the optimal treatment regimen and patient population that might benefit most from IL-21 blockade (Buniello et al., 2025; Schroderus et al., 2023).

Overall, our evaluation concludes that NNC0114-0006 merits further clinical investigation as a targeted immunomodulatory agent for T1D, particularly in early disease stages and potentially in combination with beta cell-supporting therapies. Continued research into its long-term safety profile and efficacy in preserving endogenous insulin secretion will determine its ultimate utility as part of the next generation of immune-based therapies for T1D (Buniello et al., 2025; von Herrath et al., 2021).
