β‐cell stress, whether driven by endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress or by elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS), plays a central role in type 1 diabetes (T1D) by triggering autoantigen release and neoantigen formation that promote immunogenicity and autoreactive T‐cell activation. Under conditions of heightened secretory demand or inflammatory cytokine exposure, β cells accumulate misfolded proteins and experience impaired autophagic clearance. This dysfunction leads to ER stress, which not only upregulates stress response pathways but also increases the surface expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I molecules, thereby enhancing antigen presentation (austin2025dysfunctionalβcellautophagy pages 1-2, austin2025dysfunctionalβcellautophagy pages 11-12). The perturbed protein homeostasis drives alterations in protein folding and processing, resulting in the generation of aberrant peptides that are processed into neoantigens.

These neoantigens may arise from several post‐translational modifications (PTMs) induced by cellular stress. For instance, oxidative stress within β cells promotes redox‐sensitive modifications such as cysteine‐to‐serine (C>S) substitutions in insulin, which produce novel epitopes that have been shown to be strongly antigenic (srivastava2024amicroenvironmentdrivenhlaiiassociated pages 14-16, srivastava2024amicroenvironmentdrivenhlaiiassociated pages 1-4, srivastava2024amicroenvironmentdrivenhlaiiassociated pages 11-14). Other PTMs, including citrullination, deamidation, and the formation of hybrid insulin peptides (HIPs), further diversify the peptide repertoire available for MHC binding, enabling the presentation of epitopes not encountered during thymic selection (james2018immunerecognitionof pages 4-6, james2018immunerecognitionof pages 2-3, piganelli2021theroleof pages 9-9). The cumulative effect of these molecular alterations is a shift in the immunopeptidome that favors the activation of autoreactive T cells.

Studies performed in both murine models and human β‐cell lines have confirmed that inhibition of autophagy, a key process in maintaining β‐cell homeostasis, exacerbates ER stress and enhances HLA class I expression. In human EndoC-βH1 cells, for example, autophagy blockade increased HLA-I surface levels under basal and interferon-α–stimulated conditions, linking β‐cell stress with heightened immunogenicity (austin2025dysfunctionalβcellautophagy pages 1-2, austin2025dysfunctionalβcellautophagy pages 8-11). This phenomenon is not limited to immortalized cell lines; analyses of human islets and stem cell–derived β cells suggest that stress-induced neoantigen formation is conserved across different β-cell sources, thereby potentially affecting both endogenous islets and β cells used in therapeutic transplantation (srivastava2024amicroenvironmentdrivenhlaiiassociated pages 19-21, piganelli2021theroleof pages 7-8).

The altered antigen processing associated with β-cell stress results in the presentation of neoantigens that escape central tolerance, leading to the activation and clonal expansion of autoreactive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (piganelli2021theroleof pages 4-5, piganelli2021theroleof pages 8-9). Once activated, these T cells produce inflammatory cytokines such as interferon-γ, which further upregulate MHC class I expression on β cells and create a feed-forward loop that amplifies islet immunogenicity (austin2025dysfunctionalβcellautophagy pages 11-12, austin2025dysfunctionalβcellautophagy pages 13-14). Environmental factors, including viral infections, can further exacerbate ER stress and oxidative damage, thereby intensifying the generation and presentation of neoantigens (burg2018redoxsensitiveinnateimmune pages 6-8).

In summary, the interplay between ER stress and ROS production in β cells drives the formation and release of neoantigens through redox-sensitive and stress-induced PTMs. These modifications alter peptide processing and MHC presentation, ultimately breaking immune tolerance and leading to persistent autoreactive T-cell activation in T1D. This mechanistic insight is supported by evidence in both human islets and stem cell–derived β cells, underscoring the importance of targeting β-cell stress pathways as a potential therapeutic strategy in preventing or slowing autoimmune β-cell destruction (austin2025dysfunctionalβcellautophagy pages 1-2, austin2025dysfunctionalβcellautophagy pages 11-12, pqac-1ecc9c56).
